The other codes’ answers are stored; this site’s side of those four comparisons is recomputed in your browser each time the page opens.     1The short version

    Four things are tested: how much a star dims when a planet crosses it; where the planet is on its orbit, and when; how fast the star moves toward and away from us; and whether a fit returns the right values with honest error bars.

    Every code gets identical inputs. The answers are compared with a reference computed independently of all of them (the orbit in 30-digit arithmetic, the blocked light by numerical integration), against tolerances fixed in advance.

    4The four comparisons

    Each gives a number to remember and one chart; the rest opens on request. The equations behind them are in the Methods tab: transit model, orbits, radial velocities, nested sampling.

    4.1The dimming of the star

    The fraction of starlight a planet blocks as it crosses the star, for about forty combinations of planet size, path, orbit shape and limb darkening. An error here becomes an error in the planet’s radius.

    4.2The orbit and its timing

    Where the planet is at a given time (Kepler’s equation), when it passes behind the star, and how long transits and eclipses last. Elongated orbits are the hard case: a solver that stops early puts the planet in the wrong place.

    4.3The star’s velocity

    The speed of the star along our line of sight as its planet pulls it around. Velocity codes are known for differing by a sign or by 180° in the angle ω, so the conventions are tested along with the numbers.

    4.4Fits and their error bars

    Four simulated data sets are fitted with this site’s sampler and, independently, with emcee, dynesty and UltraNest. The sampler should not matter, and a 68% interval should hold the truth 68% of the time.

    6What has not been tested

    One site in place of several programs

    An exoplanet observation usually passes through a handful of programs: a FITS viewer such as SAOImage DS9 and IRAF’s imexam to look at the frames, a photometry pipeline such as EXOTIC or AstroImageJ, a light-curve model such as batman under a sampler, a radial-velocity code such as RadVel, and the NASA Exoplanet Archive, exoplanet.eu and a transit finder to choose the target. Exoplanet Science Lab does each of these steps in the browser, with one model and one likelihood behind all of them. There is nothing to install, it runs on a phone, and the files you open never leave your computer. The same core is a Python package and an MCP server for scripts and agents.

    This page says which tool each part can stand in for, how it was checked against that tool, and where the other tool still does more. Every number is one of the Validation tab’s, which says how it was measured.

Evidence Compiled codes are faster: for the quadratic law batman and PyTransit are several times quicker. This code is faster than batman for the four-coefficient law. Timings
    Fitting and posteriors EXOTIC’s fitters, UltraNest, dynesty, emcee Nested sampling with evidence, in a Web Worker, with plain-language warnings about correlated parameters, prior edges and separate peaks. Method EXOTIC’s single-transit example: means within 0.25σ, widths within 15%, 1.8 s against 8 s; five nights at once: within 0.11σ, 4.2 s against 57 s. The hard problems against UltraNest 4.6.3. Evidence · sampler UltraNest, dynesty and emcee have more options and much longer publication records; for a paper, check a result with one of them.
    Radial velocities and joint fits RadVel, pyaneti, EXOTIC Several planets and instruments, jitter, trends, transits, velocities and transit times in one fit; the Rossiter–McLaughlin effect. Evidence · orbits RadVel and pyaneti are established in the literature, with their own Gaussian-process models for stellar activity.
    Timing variations and N-body REBOUND Wisdom–Holman and IAS15 integrators, photodynamical light curves, a search for an unseen companion. Method Transit times equal REBOUND 5.2.2’s IAS15 to 1.3e-6 s; positions equal WHFast to 2e-12 AU. Evidence REBOUND has many more integrators, collisions and forces, and handles large numbers of bodies.

    Why use it alongside them

    • Nothing to install. Open the page. The Python package installs from GitHub with pip and needs three packages, all with prebuilt wheels: numpy, pydantic and a JavaScript engine that runs the same core as the site.
    • One model throughout. Photometry, fitting, planning and the catalogue share the same transit model and likelihood, so a comparison star is chosen by how well the light curve fits, not by a separate score.
    • Private by default. Images and data are read in the browser and are not uploaded anywhere.
    • Checked in the open. Each comparison above is a test in the repository against the other program’s own output; the Validation tab lists them, including where this code loses.
